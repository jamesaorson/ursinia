## Chapter 5

- (v. 2-5) 2 Through him we have also obtained access by faith into this grace in which we stand, and we rejoice in hope
  of the glory of God. 3 Not only that, but we rejoice in our sufferings, knowing that suffering produces endurance, 4 and
  endurance produces character, and character produces hope, 5 and hope does not put us to shame, because God's love has
  been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us.
  - Paul lays out our reasons for joy and the natural results of this joy.
    - Access to grace gives us joy
    - Joy is had even in our suffering
    - For suffering produces endurance (James 1:2-4)
    - Endurance produces character (trustworthiness)
    - And that character produces hope
  - We are now able to love properly, for we now have the love of God within us, which could not be said before we had faith.
    - All man experiences some degree of God’s love, no matter his state.
    - However, the common experiential love of man is denoted by “common grace” or “common providence”, in that the Lord
      provides some good things to all man, such as breath unto life.
- (v. 6) 6 For while we were still weak, at the right time Christ died for the ungodly.
  - “At the right time”
    - Man often questions why God did not save us at the beginning of time.
    - A similar question is: “Why was Adam allowed to sin in the first place?”
    - It is not only the right time that Christ was sacrificed for us, but even having “a time” that He would be sacrificed
      was good.
      - The fall was ordained unto a good end.
      - The redemption of man by Christ’s sacrifice was the most good end.
- (v. 7-11) 7 For one will scarcely die for a righteous person—though perhaps for a good person one would dare even to die—8
  but God shows his love for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us. 9 Since, therefore, we have now
  been justified by his blood, much more shall we be saved by him from the wrath of God. 10 For if while we were enemies
  we were reconciled to God by the death of his Son, much more, now that we are reconciled, shall we be saved by his life.
  11 More than that, we also rejoice in God through our Lord Jesus Christ, through whom we have now received reconciliation.
  - Focusing upon verses 10-11, we should recognize the deep realities of His love for the elect.
    - Even while we were sinners, He did this act for us that we may be saved.
    - Now that we are justified and made right before Him, how should we relate to God now, and what should we expect from
      Him?
      - We should rejoice in proportion to the sin of which we have been released.
        - We have been released from unpayable debt.
        - We have been released from eternal punishment.
        - We have been granted an eternal reward.
        - We should work to rejoice in every action, motive, and thought.
      - We can and should expect much more than the common graces of man.
        - Release from sin, if we come to him with genuinely repentant hearts.
        - Great gifts from God, those gifts which work to glorify the Lord and further His church.
        - Answered prayers
- (v. 12) 12 Therefore, just as sin came into the world through one man, and death through sin, and so death spread to
  all men because all sinned—
  - All who are counted as “man” have sinned.
  - This passage should make difficult the dogma of the “Immaculate Conception” for the Romanist,
    ([RCC 484-511, especially 491](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_one/section_two/chapter_two/artcile_3/paragraph_2_conceived_by_the_power_of_the_holy_spirit_and_born_of_the_virgin_mary.html)).
    - Rejection:
      - Mary was of man, for she had an earthly father.
      - Mary would therefore have the stain of original sin.
      - It is illogical to claim that Mary could be born of man, without stain or original sin, in order to prerequisite
        Christ’s birth without original sin.
        - Christ was born of the heavenly Father and God Himself, meaning His federal head was not Adam.
        - Therefore Christ was born without the stain of original sin.
- (v 13-14) 13 for sin indeed was in the world before the law was given, but sin is not counted where there is no law.
  14 Yet death reigned from Adam to Moses, even over those whose sinning was not like the transgression of Adam, who was
  a type of the one who was to come.
  - Paul reminds us that sin is only present if there is law, and death can only reign where there is sin. So how could
    there be death before Moses, if the law is that which was given to Moses?
    - The Law therefore must precede the law given to Moses, for death reigned.
    - This accounts for natural law given to all man, and not just the positive commands given to Adam and Moses.
    - This is a piece of evidence useful in refutation of the antinomian notion that even the 10 commandments are law given
      only to Moses, and that has been fully abrogated along with the civil and ceremonial law given to Moses.
      - Rather, Paul helps us to recognize that there must be some transcendent law present which man continues to transgress
        in that time between Adam and Moses.
      - We can also see this in the sin of Cain and in the Noahic Covenant.
        - Cain sinned, yet no law had been physically given (as we can yet see recorded in scripture). God still expected
          him to not only know that he should not murder, but even expected Cain to know that he must provide a proper tribute
          for his own sin.
        - Noah is given instruction on how to deal with murderers, and is given permission to eat of the flesh of animals,
          also implying to us that this was not permitted before this covenant with Noah, again demonstrating a law of some
          kind given to man.
      - Therefore, man has trespassed against a natural law of God before the law was given to Moses, not requiring this
        law to be written upon stone, but it is sufficient that this law has been written on the hearts of man.
- (v. 15, 17) 15 But the free gift is not like the trespass. For if many died through one man's trespass, much more have
  the grace of God and the free gift by the grace of that one man Jesus Christ abounded for many. 17 For if, because of
  one man’s trespass, death reigned through that one man, much more will those who receive the abundance of grace and the
  free gift of righteousness reign in life through the one man Jesus Christ.
  - To the universalist, on first look, verse 15 presents supporting evidence, however upon further review into verse 17,
    it actually argues against universal salvation.
    - If “many” have died through one man’s trespass, and that “many” is equivalent to “all man”, then how can the grace
      of God apply to “much more”?
      - If the whole set of man is included, how can grace apply to “much more” than the whole set?
      - So the phrase “much more” must not refer to “more people”, but be in reference to the magnitude of the gift when
        compared to the judgment of man.
        - I should spend some time later considering the implications of this.
        - In what way is God’s judgment “less” than His reward and mercy?
        - May not be a proper way of stating things.
- (v. 19) 19 For as by the one man’s disobedience the many were made sinners, so by the one man’s obedience the many will
  be made righteous.
  - Christ is that one man who was obedient.
  - No man has ever been truly obedient other than Christ.
  - Only those who then make Christ their head receive the righteousness imputed.
    - Who receives Christ as their head?
    - The elect who necessarily believe.
- (v. 20-21) 20 Now the law came in to increase the trespass, but where sin increased, grace abounded all the more, 21 so
  that, as sin reigned in death, grace also might reign through righteousness leading to eternal life through Jesus Christ
  our Lord.
  - Before faith, we only increase in sin.
    - For the unelect, their sin increases so God’s justice can be more greatly displayed through judgment. This can be
      understood by mapping to human rendering of justice upon greatly evil men. We feel a grand achievement and relief
      in seeing great evils quelled, and all the more is this feeling increased when an eternal God acts out perfect judgment
      upon evil men.
    - For the elect, grace abounds in the sin we have committed, and the debt we are saved from is made greater, increasing
      our experience of His mercy in forgiveness.

## Chapter 6

- (v. 1-2) 1 What shall we say then? Are we to continue in sin that grace may abound? 2 By no means! How can we who died
  to sin still live in it?
  - Christ has covered us with His righteousness, but sin is still a reality for man. He has not abrogated the law insomuch
    as He has also deleted the effects of the law, which is the charging of sin upon man.
  - Grace abounds to an equal measure of the sin it covers, further glorifying the Lord in His mercy.
    - The one who is justified does not desire to further harm His Lord.
    - Despite the truth that greater forgiveness is a greater mercy given by the Lord, how much better is it for the believer
      to glorify the Lord through good works?
    - Therefore, we rest in the knowledge that God’s grace abounds in our sin, however, we should not act to intentionally
      increase the grace that God gives to us, rather desiring good works to bless Him.
- (v. 3-4) 3 Do you not know that all of us who have been baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into his death? 4 We
  were buried therefore with him by baptism into death, in order that, just as Christ was raised from the dead by the glory
  of the Father, we too might walk in newness of life.
  - We have died to sin and been raised up like Christ, partially glorified now through the indwelling of the Holy Spirit.
  - Therefore, we should not work to grieve the Holy Spirit through actions of death, recognizing we have life that we lacked
    before.
- (v. 6-11) 6 We know that our old self was crucified with him in order that the body of sin might be brought to nothing,
  so that we would no longer be enslaved to sin. 7 For one who has died has been set free from sin. 8 Now if we have died
  with Christ, we believe that we will also live with him. 9 We know that Christ, being raised from the dead, will never
  die again; death no longer has dominion over him. 10 For the death he died he died to sin, once for all, but the life
  he lives he lives to God. 11 So you also must consider yourselves dead to sin and alive to God in Christ Jesus.
  - We are set free from sin, so why do we still sin?
    - Misunderstanding of this passage has led to Wesleyan Christian Perfection theology, where it is believed Christians
      can be without sin in this life.
    - Rather, we should understand this as telling us we are no longer bound (enslaved) to sin, and therefore are free to
      act without sinning.
      - But we are still not raised in new bodies yet.
      - Our bond to sin is broken; but our bodies still are broken by sin and the effects of the fall are still evident.
    - Again, our justification is not that we are righteous, but we are covered by Christ’s righteousness. Not until we
      are glorified can we claim that we can no longer sin.
    - We are dead to sin, but sin is not yet dead in us.
  - We can now do good, and are alive in Christ, for before we never could do good works.
- (v. 14-16) 14 For sin will have no dominion over you, since you are not under law but under grace. 15 What then? Are we
  to sin because we are not under law but under grace? By no means! 16 Do you not know that if you present yourselves to
  anyone as obedient slaves, you are slaves of the one whom you obey, either of sin, which leads to death, or of obedience,
  which leads to righteousness?
  - The antinomian likes to say that because of verse 14, we have no need for the law.
  - Paul clarifies, for the reader, that the law is clearly still relevant, for the one who disregards God’s law demonstrates
    he is still a slave to sin.
    - This does not mean that sinning at all demonstrates one is a slave to sin.
    - Rather, one who sins and does not have a heart of repentance recognizing he has broken God’s law, is still a slave
      to sin.
- (v. 20-23) 20 For when you were slaves of sin, you were free in regard to righteousness. 21 But what fruit were you getting
  at that time from the things of which you are now ashamed? For the end of those things is death. 22 But now that you have
  been set free from sin and have become slaves of God, the fruit you get leads to sanctification and its end, eternal life.
  23 For the wages of sin is death, but the free gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord.
  - Our relationship has shifted
    - We are no longer slaves to sin
    - We are rather slaves to God
    - This means we serve him, and we bear fruit on his behalf, to benefit and lead to our sanctification
  - Consider how often we see “bearing fruit” used in scripture as an analog for “serving” the Lord
    - Look at Genesis, for God gave fruit and a command as the object by which Adam and Eve were to demonstrate obedience
    - Now, in the New Covenant, we bear fruit by the Spirit, and bear fruit in growing the church
    - Think of the “wicked tenants” parable

## Chapter 7

- (v. 1-4) 1 Or do you not know, brothers—for I am speaking to those who know the law—that the law is binding on a person
  only as long as he lives? 2 For a married woman is bound by law to her husband while he lives, but if her husband dies
  she is released from the law of marriage. 3 Accordingly, she will be called an adulteress if she lives with another man
  while her husband is alive. But if her husband dies, she is free from that law, and if she marries another man she is
  not an adulteress. 4 Likewise, my brothers, you also have died to the law through the body of Christ, so that you may
  belong to another, to him who has been raised from the dead, in order that we may bear fruit for God.
  - We are buried in baptism and raised into new life, and it is in this death to the old self that we die to the law.
    - The baptism itself does not make us Christ’s
    - The baptism is a symbol that we are ones who belong to Christ
  - Again, “that we may bear fruit” signifies our work in the church as “fruit-bearing”, again
- (v. 5-6) 5 For while we were living in the flesh, our sinful passions, aroused by the law, were at work in our members
  to bear fruit for death. 6 But now we are released from the law, having died to that which held us captive, so that we
  serve in the new way of the Spirit and not in the old way of the written code.
  - We are not bound by the law of Moses, but bound instead to the law of the Spirit
    - Which allows us to serve and grow in observance of God’s eternal law
    - For we are not saved by our service, for we are only saved by the free grace and imputation of Christ’s righteousness,
      but rather we grow in the Spirit and in love for God’s law.
- (v. 7) 7 What then shall we say? That the law is sin? By no means! Yet if it had not been for the law, I would not have
  known sin. For I would not have known what it is to covet if the law had not said, “You shall not covet.”
  - Notice that Paul refers to the tenth commandment here when demonstrating how the law reveals to him his sin as a mirror
    - This is not an arbitrary choosing, but rather the tenth commandment encompasses the whole of the law
    - Consider the commandments before the tenth, and ask yourself “what does the tenth add?”
      - The answer is “nothing”,
      - Except for an explanation/commentary of all the commandments prior
      - We covet our neighbor’s possessions, their wives, their lives, our parent’s position of honor, our time on the sabbath,
        and even our Lord’s position over us.
- (v. 9-13 BSB) 9 Once I was alive apart from the law; but when the commandment came, sin sprang to life and I died.
  10 So I discovered that the very commandment that was meant to bring life actually brought death. 11 For sin, seizing
  its opportunity through the commandment, deceived me and through the commandment put me to death 12 So then, the law
  is holy, and the commandment is holy, righteous, and good. 13 Did that which is good, then, become death to me? Certainly
  not! But in order that sin might be exposed as sin, it produced death in me through what was good, so that through the
  commandment sin might become utterly sinful.
  - It is not that the law itself is death
    - The law made it possible for sin to come in and bring death upon man.
    - Sin is the source of death, while the law acts as the judge and sentencer of death
    - Since the Mosaic covenant was not of the same substance as the covenant of grace, it can be properly called a commandment
      that brought death.
      - It shined a light on the latent sin of man
      - It made Israel accountable by giving them positive laws to follow (John 9:41)
    - The Mosaic covenant does indeed contain the Ten commandments, but Paul is not referring alone to the Ten commandments
      for his point. He is speaking to the entirety of the Mosaic law, and even that which is abrogated was still good for
      it was a command of the Lord.
- (v. 14) 14 We know that the law is spiritual; but I am unspiritual, sold as a slave to sin.
  - “unspiritual” here can be translated as “fleshly”, so Paul is affirming that the law is of God and spiritual, not physical.
    - There is a question of which law he is referring to here. It makes sense it could be the eternal moral law because
      he is using a different word ([νόμος](https://biblehub.com/greek/3551.htm)) from what we translate as commandment
      earlier ([ἐντολῆς](https://biblehub.com/greek/1785.htm)).
      - There is the matter of verse 12 where both are used.
      - Part of what we can do is see that verse 12 lists 3 adjectives to describe the commandments, meaning this is referring
        to the threefold division of the whole law.
    - So we can therefore say the whole of God’s law is spiritual, not just the eternal moral law.
    - Spiritual does not equal eternal though, as angels are not eternal and yet are spirit.
- (v. 15-17) 15 I do not understand what I do. For what I want to do, I do not do. But what I hate, I do. 16 And if I do
  what I do not want to do, I admit that the law is good. 17 In that case, it is no longer I who do it, but it is sin living
  in me that does it.
  - Paul was a slave to sin, but while now regenerate he still does those things which he hates.
    - Why does he hate what he does though? It is because the law is good, and he states this immediately after.
    - If the law were not good, he would not hate that which he does when he sins against it.
    - Which leads him to conclude that there is still sin within him.
    - Consider 1 John 3:9: 9 Anyone born of God refuses to practice sin, because God’s seed abides in him; he cannot go
      on sinning, because he has been born of God.
      - How can these co-exist?
      - The answer is in verse 17, that it is sin that does it, not the man who hates the sin and wills against it.
- (v. 18-20) 18 I know that nothing good lives in me, that is, in my flesh; for I have the desire to do what is good, but
  I cannot carry it out. 19 For I do not do the good I want to do. Instead, I keep on doing the evil I do not want to do.
  20 And if I do what I do not want, it is no longer I who do it, but it is sin living in me that does it.
  - Flesh lives in our bodies, and as long as we are in our flesh we should expect this to be our condition.
    - Therefore, the Wesleyan doctrine of Christian perfection is nonsensical, for we are bound to our flesh until we are
      glorified with new bodies.
- (v. 21-23) 21 So this is the principle I have discovered: When I want to do good, evil is right there with me. 22 For
  in my inner being I delight in God’s law. 23 But I see another law at work in my body, warring against the law of my mind
  and holding me captive to the law of sin that dwells within me.
  - We do actually desire good when we are regenerated, even as we war with sin.
- (v. 24) 24 What a wretched man I am! Who will rescue me from this body of death?
  - Christ is the only one who can rescue us, but until our body endures the death brought on by sin we cannot be rescued.
    We must follow in the footsteps of our Lord and be raised up from the dead to new life.
- (v. 25) 25 Thanks be to God, through Jesus Christ our Lord! So then, with my mind I serve the law of God, but with my
  flesh I serve the law of sin.
  - Our minds should be set upon Christ, serving Him, and loving God’s law, continually conquering our flesh with repentance.
