1. Samuel dies and all Israel mourns him (v. 1).
   - His death is noted without ceremony — one verse — before the narrative moves on.
   - The last prophet who anointed Saul and then David is gone. The word of the LORD has already been spoken; it now works itself out without him.
1. The narrator introduces Nabal and Abigail before the conflict begins (vv. 2–3).
   - Nabal: wealthy, Calebite, harsh, badly behaved. His name means "fool."
   - Abigail: discerning and beautiful.
   - The author has told you the whole story in two sentences.
1. David sends ten men to Nabal at sheep-shearing with a careful, respectful request (vv. 4–9).
   - Sheep-shearing is a time of celebration and customary generosity. The ask is reasonable.
   - David frames his request around service rendered: his men had been a wall of protection around Nabal's shepherds in the wilderness.
   - The greeting is elaborate and honorable — David is not demanding; he is asking.
1. Nabal responds with contempt (vv. 10–11).
   - "Who is David? Who is the son of Jesse?" — he denies David's identity, his legitimacy, and his service in one breath.
   - He compares David to a runaway slave.
   - He will not give what is customary to men he claims not to know — but he knows exactly who David is.
1. David arms four hundred men and rides to destroy Nabal's household (vv. 12–13).
   - The restraint of ch. 24 nearly collapses here. The insult strikes at David's identity and his men's honor.
   - David swears to kill every male in Nabal's house by morning (v. 22). This would be bloodguilt — vengeance taken into his own hands, innocent men killed alongside the guilty.
1. Abigail acts without hesitation and without telling Nabal (vv. 14–19).
   - One of Nabal's servants brings her the report and names Nabal plainly: "evil is determined against our master and against all his house, for he is such a worthless man that one cannot speak to him" (v. 17).
   - Abigail loads donkeys with provisions and rides out to intercept David — she does not wait for permission she will not receive.
1. Abigail's speech is the theological center of the chapter (vv. 24–31).
   - She takes the blame on herself, buying space for David to hear her.
   - She calls Nabal by his name and nature: "For as his name is, so is he. Nabal is his name, and folly is with him" (v. 25).
   - She points David away from vengeance and toward his future: the LORD will make him a sure house; his life is bound in the bundle of the living with the LORD; his enemies will be slung out like a stone from a sling.
   - Her argument is not merely practical but theological: do not take vengeance yourself and create a cause for grief or stumbling when you are king (v. 31).
1. David blesses Abigail and accepts her restraint (vv. 32–35).
   - "Blessed be the LORD, the God of Israel, who sent you this day to meet me! Blessed be your discretion, and blessed be you, who have kept me this day from bloodguilt."
   - David recognizes in Abigail what he recognized in himself in ch. 24: the LORD's hand checking a man before he sins.
1. The LORD himself strikes Nabal (vv. 36–38).
   - Nabal is feasting like a king, drunk, when Abigail returns. She waits until morning to tell him.
   - When she does, his heart dies within him and he becomes like a stone. Ten days later the LORD strikes him and he dies.
   - David did not need to act. The LORD avenged the insult without David incurring bloodguilt — the same principle as ch. 24.
1. David takes Abigail as his wife; Saul has given Michal to another man (vv. 39–44).
   - Abigail accepts immediately and with humility.
   - The note about Michal is pointed: Saul continues to work against David even indirectly, stripping him of the wife God gave him through the kingship.
