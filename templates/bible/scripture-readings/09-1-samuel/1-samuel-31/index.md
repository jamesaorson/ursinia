1. The Philistines rout Israel; Saul's three sons — Jonathan, Abinadab, and Malchishua — fall on Mount Gilboa (vv. 1–2).
   - Jonathan, David's covenant friend and faithful man, dies here beside his father. He does not share his father's guilt, but he shares his fate.
   - The sons of the king die with the king — the dynasty ends here, as God said it would.
1. Saul, critically wounded by archers, asks his armor-bearer to run him through so that the uncircumcised won't abuse him; the armor-bearer refuses; Saul falls on his own sword (vv. 3–5).
   - The armor-bearer's refusal is a final act of reverence for the LORD's anointed, even a failed one.
   - Saul cannot trust God in this moment any more than he could in any other. He will not wait. He takes his death into his own hands.
   - This is the fitting end of a man who, his whole reign, tried to manage God's will rather than submit to it.
1. The word of the LORD through Samuel is fulfilled exactly (v. 6; cf. 28:19).
   - Samuel told Saul: "Tomorrow you and your sons shall be with me." It happens as spoken.
   - God's word does not return empty. The long delay between the sentence (ch. 13, 15) and its execution (ch. 31) does not mean the word has failed — it means God is patient in his judgments.
1. Israel flees; the Philistines occupy the abandoned cities (v. 7).
   - The national consequence of the king's sin falls on the whole people.
   - This is how covenant life works: the head's faithfulness or failure shapes the fortunes of those under him.
1. The Philistines strip Saul's armor, cut off his head, and fasten his body to the wall of Beth-shan (vv. 8–10).
   - They put his armor in the temple of Ashtaroth and announce the victory in their idol-temples — the exact humiliation Saul feared.
   - The man who would not be humbled before God is humiliated before the nations.
1. The men of Jabesh-gilead travel through the night to recover the bodies, bury the bones, and fast seven days (vv. 11–13).
   - These men have not forgotten: Saul's first act as king was to save Jabesh-gilead from the Ammonites (ch. 11). They honor the memory of that man.
   - This is the only act of faithfulness and steadfast love at the close of Saul's story — and it comes not from his court or his army, but from a city on the fringe he saved decades before.
   - The book ends not with triumph, but with a decent burial and a week of mourning. The first king of Israel closes in grief and shame, his body rescued from a pagan wall.
