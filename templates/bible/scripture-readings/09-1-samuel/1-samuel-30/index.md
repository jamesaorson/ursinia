1. David returns to Ziklag to find it burned and all the women and children taken by Amalekite raiders (vv. 1–5).
   - The very people Saul was ordered to destroy (ch. 15) and spared are now the instrument of David's suffering.
   - David and his men weep until they have no strength left to weep.
1. David's men speak of stoning him; David strengthens himself in the LORD his God (v. 6).
   - This moment is the sharpest contrast to Saul in the entire book: in extremity Saul turns to a medium (ch. 28); David turns to God.
   - The word "strengthened himself" (הִתְחַזֵּק) implies an act of will and faith, not a feeling — David chooses to rest on God when everything around him has collapsed.
1. David inquires of the LORD through Abiathar and the ephod; God commands him to pursue and promises full recovery (vv. 7–8).
   - The ephod is with David because Abiathar — the last priest of Nob — fled to him (ch. 22). Saul's rejection of the means of grace has consequences.
   - God's answer is precise: "you shall surely recover all." Not partial, not probably — all.
1. Providence operates through an unexpected instrument: an abandoned Egyptian slave leads David to the Amalekite camp (vv. 11–15).
   - The Amalekite master discarded him when he fell sick, but God uses him to bring David exactly where he needs to go.
   - This is God's common way: the weak and discarded become the hinge of his purposes.
1. David attacks and recovers everything — wives, children, cattle, and all the plunder — nothing was missing (vv. 17–20).
   - The promise of v. 8 is fulfilled to the letter.
   - David also recovers the spoil the Amalekites had taken from Philistia and Judah in the same raid — he returns with more than he lost.
1. David rules that the 200 men too exhausted to cross the Besor Brook share equally in the spoil with those who fought (vv. 22–25).
   - Some of David's men call the 200 "wicked and worthless men" and would give them only their wives — David rejects this.
   - "For as his share is who goes down into the battle, so shall his share be who stays by the supplies; they shall share alike."
   - This ruling becomes a statute in Israel. David, not yet king, is already legislating justice and mercy.
1. David distributes portions of the spoil to the elders of Judah in every city he and his men had roamed (vv. 26–31).
   - This is not merely generosity — it is the political groundwork of a future king who has spent years among his people and remembers them.
   - He calls it "a present for you from the spoil of the enemies of the LORD" — not his gift, but the LORD's victory shared.
