1. David flees to the cave of Adullam, and there a company gathers around him (vv. 1–2).
   - His brothers and his father's house come down to him — the family that once overlooked him now shelters under him.
   - Four hundred men join him: distressed, indebted, discontented. Society's outcasts become the nucleus of the future king's army.
   - This is a pattern the LORD repeats: the anointed king is surrounded not by the powerful but by the broken. Christ gathers sinners.
1. David places his parents in the care of the king of Moab (vv. 3–4).
   - David's great-grandmother was Ruth the Moabitess; there is a kinship link here, however thin.
   - Even as a fugitive, David is responsible and protective — he does not drag his elderly parents into the wilderness.
1. The prophet Gad commands David to leave the stronghold and return to Judah (v. 5).
   - David is not acting alone or by instinct; he is under prophetic direction.
   - He obeys immediately. The contrast with Saul, who ignored Samuel, is deliberate.
1. Saul's court becomes a scene of paranoid accusation (vv. 6–8).
   - He is seated under the tamarisk tree at Gibeah, spear in hand — the same spear he twice hurled at David, and once at Jonathan.
   - His appeal is tribal and transactional: "Is the son of Jesse giving you fields and vineyards?" He has nothing to offer but patronage politics.
   - He accuses his entire court of conspiracy and frames Jonathan's covenant with David as treason.
1. Doeg the Edomite informs on Ahimelech and Nob (vv. 9–10).
   - The seed planted by David's lie in chapter 21 now bears its fruit. Doeg was there; David knew it (v. 22). The lie created a witness.
1. Ahimelech's defense before Saul is honest, faithful, and perfectly reasoned (vv. 14–15).
   - He asks who among all Saul's servants is as loyal as David. He acted in good faith, knowing nothing of any rift between the king and his son-in-law.
   - His innocence is transparent. Saul ignores it entirely.
1. Saul orders the slaughter of the priests; his own guards refuse (v. 17).
   - Even Saul's servants will not strike the priests of the LORD. There is a line they will not cross.
   - Doeg the Edomite has no such restraint. He kills eighty-five men who wore the linen ephod.
1. Doeg destroys Nob entirely — men, women, children, infants, and livestock (vv. 18–19).
   - This is one of the most horrifying acts in the book of Samuel. A city of priests, obliterated.
   - It fulfills the word of the man of God against Eli's house in 1 Samuel 2:31–33: "the arm of your father's house" cut off, none reaching old age.
   - Saul, who would not destroy Amalek as the LORD commanded, destroys the LORD's own priests on his own initiative. The inversion is damning.
1. Abiathar escapes and comes to David; David takes full responsibility (vv. 20–23).
   - "I knew that Doeg the Edomite was there that day… I myself am responsible for the lives of everyone in your father's house."
   - David does not hedge or minimize. He caused this, and he owns it.
   - He makes Abiathar a promise: "He who seeks your life is seeking mine as well. You will be safe with me."
   - The last surviving priest of Nob is sheltered under the LORD's anointed — and brings with him the ephod, the means of inquiring of God.
