1. Saul takes three thousand chosen men and pursues David into the wilderness of En-gedi (vv. 1–2).
   - David has just escaped Saul's hand when the Philistine raid pulled Saul away (ch. 23). The pursuit resumes immediately — Saul cannot stop.
   - The contrast is stark: while David has been fighting Israel's enemies, Saul has been hunting Israel's future king.
1. Saul enters a cave to relieve himself; David and his men are sitting in the very back of it (v. 3).
   - David's men read the moment as divine appointment: "This is the day the LORD said to you, 'Behold, I will give your enemy into your hand'" (v. 4).
   - They interpret circumstances as divine permission — a pattern Saul himself is guilty of (ch. 23:7). David does not.
1. David cuts the corner of Saul's robe, and immediately his heart strikes him (vv. 4–5).
   - He does not kill. He does not wound. He takes only a scrap of cloth — and even that troubles him.
   - His conscience is more sensitive to the LORD's anointed than his men's theology of opportunity is.
   - "The LORD forbid that I should do this thing to my lord, the LORD's anointed, to put out my hand against him, seeing he is the LORD's anointed" (v. 6).
1. David restrains his men and lets Saul leave the cave (v. 7).
   - He does not merely refrain himself — he commands his men not to act. The restraint is complete.
   - Spiritual discipline here is not passive. David actively prevents the opportunity from becoming a decision.
1. David calls after Saul, bows to the ground, and makes his case (vv. 8–15).
   - He shows the corner of the robe as evidence of his innocence and his restraint.
   - He appeals to the LORD to judge between them: "my hand shall not be against you" (v. 12).
   - He quotes the proverb: "Out of the wicked comes wickedness" (v. 13) — he is saying that what he did today proves he is not the threat Saul imagines.
   - He identifies himself as a dead dog, a flea — nothing Saul needs to fear (v. 14).
1. Saul weeps and confesses what he cannot deny (vv. 16–19).
   - "You are more righteous than I, for you have repaid me good, whereas I have repaid you evil."
   - He asks the question that answers itself: "If a man finds his enemy, will he let him go away safe?" He knows what David did. He cannot explain it by anything other than the righteousness of the man.
1. Saul acknowledges that David will be king and asks him to swear not to destroy his house (vv. 20–22).
   - Even Saul's own mouth now confesses what the LORD has said since ch. 15. His confession does not change his heart — he returns to hunting David again in ch. 26.
   - David swears. He holds himself to this oath even after Saul's death (2 Samuel 9).
