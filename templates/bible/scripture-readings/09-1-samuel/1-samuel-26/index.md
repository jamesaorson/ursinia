1. The Ziphites betray David's location to Saul a second time (v. 1), and Saul assembles three thousand men and marches out (v. 2).
   - This is the same group that betrayed David in ch. 23, and Saul is camped near the same hill of Hachilah.
   - The narrator's repetition is intentional: we are about to see whether David has learned anything, and whether Saul has.
1. David sends spies, confirms Saul has come, and goes down to Saul's camp at night with Abishai (vv. 4–7).
   - Abishai is introduced here for the first time — Joab's brother, son of Zeruiah — and he will be a recurring figure in David's story.
1. Abishai reads the situation as divine appointment, exactly as David's men did in the cave (v. 8; cf. 24:4).
   - He offers to pin Saul to the ground with his own spear in one stroke.
   - The temptation is the same as before, but more urgent — they are in the open camp, surrounded.
1. David refuses on the same theological grounds: the LORD's anointed may not be destroyed by a man's hand (v. 9).
   - But his reasoning is now sharper: "The LORD shall smite him; or his day shall come to die; or he shall descend into battle, and perish" (v. 10).
   - David names three possible ends for Saul, none of which involve David. He has fully internalized that vengeance belongs to the LORD.
1. The LORD causes a deep sleep to fall on the entire camp (v. 12).
   - This is not merely good fortune. The narrator is explicit that God sovereignly prevented David from sinning, even while David was the one who chose to enter the danger.
   - David takes Saul's spear and water cruse — the same kind of token he took in the cave — and withdraws.
1. From a safe distance David shouts across to Abner, rebuking him for failing to guard the LORD's anointed (vv. 14–16).
   - The irony is pointed: the man who refuses to harm Saul is the one rebuking Saul's own general for not protecting him.
1. Saul recognizes David's voice and confesses yet again (vv. 17–21).
   - "I have played the fool, and have erred exceedingly" (v. 21) — Saul now names himself with the same word used for Nabal in ch. 25.
   - Both Saul and Nabal are fools who will be dealt with by the LORD, not by David's hand.
1. David articulates the principle that closes the exchange (vv. 23–24): the LORD renders to every man his righteousness and faithfulness.
   - David did not reach out his hand against the LORD's anointed; therefore he calls on the LORD to set his own life at equal value.
   - This is not bargaining — it is covenant reasoning.
1. Saul blesses David and predicts his success (v. 25), then they each go their own way.
   - Unlike ch. 24, there is no weeping, no covenant. Saul is running out of ways to respond.
   - David does not go back. He has learned not to trust the moment of Saul's remorse.
