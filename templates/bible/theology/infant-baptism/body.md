A historical and theological study of how paedobaptism — the baptism of infants — became the default
practice of the church, and why believers' baptism returned.

The question is not primarily "who is right?" That question is settled for this project by the
[1689 Second London Baptist Confession](https://www.1689.com/confession.html), ch. 29. The question is
_how the change happened_: by what mechanism a church that (on the evidence) baptized professing adults
by immersion came, over roughly four centuries, to baptize infants as a matter of course — and then to
treat that practice as apostolic.

## Scope

1. **The starting point.** H. F. Stander and J. P. Louw, _Baptism in the Early Church_ — the case that
   pre-Nicene baptism was immersion of professing believers, and that the patristic evidence for infant
   baptism is later, thinner, and more contested than the standard account allows.
2. **The mechanism.** Not "who argued for it" but what made it _plausible_: the doctrine of original sin,
   infant mortality rates, the theology of baptismal regeneration, the emergency-baptism instinct, the
   shift from persecuted sect to imperial religion, and the collapse of the adult catechumenate.
3. **The distinctives.** Paedobaptists do not agree with each other about _why_ they baptize infants.
   Roman sacramental efficacy, Lutheran infant faith, Reformed covenantal sign, and Anglican hybrid are
   four different arguments that happen to produce the same act. Collapsing them is the standard Baptist
   polemical error and this project will not do it.
4. **The return.** Why believers' baptism reappeared in the 1520s, why it was killed by both Catholics and
   Magisterial Reformers, and how it survived into the English Baptist tradition and then into global
   majority practice.

## Thesis Direction

The hypothesis under examination: **infant baptism is not an apostolic practice that later required
justification, but a pastoral practice that generated its own justification retroactively — and the
justifications offered are mutually incompatible.**

Three sub-claims carry the weight:

1. **Chronology.** The earliest unambiguous evidence for infant baptism is Tertullian's objection to it
   (c. 200), which proves the practice existed and was contested. Universal infant baptism is a
   fifth-century settlement, not a first-century one.
2. **Causation.** The decisive engine was the marriage of baptismal regeneration to original sin, finalized
   by Augustine against the Pelagians. Once baptism _removes_ guilt and unbaptized infants are _lost_, delay
   becomes cruelty. The argument is pastoral before it is exegetical.
3. **Incoherence.** The Reformed covenantal defense (Calvin, Westminster) is a _replacement_ argument, not
   the historic one. It was constructed in the sixteenth century to keep the practice after discarding the
   sacramental mechanism that produced it. That the practice survived its own rationale is the single most
   interesting fact in this study.

The honest counterweight, which the paper must carry rather than dodge: the covenantal argument is not
_ad hoc_ merely because it is late. Circumcision-baptism typology in Col. 2:11–12 is genuine exegetical
ground, and the Baptist has to answer the continuity of the covenant of grace, not just the absence of
explicit infant baptisms in Acts.
